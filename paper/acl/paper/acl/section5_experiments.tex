\section{Experiments}\label{sec:experiments}

We validate the claims of Sections~\ref{sec:diagonal-dominance}--\ref{sec:verification}: fingerprint existence (Section~\ref{sec:exp-exists}), lineage verification on language models (Section~\ref{sec:exp-verify}), the distinction between behavioral and weight ancestry (Section~\ref{sec:exp-distill}), robustness under reparameterization (Section~\ref{sec:exp-baselines}), and a public-checkpoint case study (Section~\ref{sec:exp-llm}).

\subsection{The Fingerprint Exists and Is Learned}\label{sec:exp-exists}

Section~\ref{sec:mechanism} predicts that the fingerprint is learned, requires the skip connection, and arises from gradient coupling rather than isometry. We test all three.

We train depth-24 residual MLPs (in\_dim$=$24, hidden$=$64) on CIFAR-10~\citep{krizhevsky2009cifar} for 200 epochs (3 seeds) under seven initialization schemes: orthogonal, Kaiming-normal/uniform, Xavier-normal/uniform, uniform, and Gaussian $\sigma{=}0.02$. Input-output block pairing accuracy starts at chance (${\leq}2.1\%$) and reaches 93--100\% after training for every scheme that converges (Appendix~\ref{app:init-ablation}); the chance-level baseline follows from a null model in which all pairs score uniformly (Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}). Orthogonal initialization satisfies dynamical isometry exactly at initialization yet yields zero correct pairs, confirming that the fingerprint requires training.

Using the same architecture, we compare ResNet-24 against PlainNet-24, which removes only the skip connection. ResNet-24 reaches 100\% pair accuracy (AUROC 0.98); PlainNet-24, trained to similar loss (1.56 vs.\ 1.35), stays at chance (3\%, AUROC 0.51). Training alone does not produce the fingerprint; the skip connection is required.

Then, we measure the normalized Jacobian deviation $\delta_J^{\mathrm{norm}} = \|J^\top J/\|J\|_F^2 - I/d\|_F$ across the GPT-2 family (small/medium/large/XL), comparing pretrained weights against random initialization. Trained GPT-2 Jacobians are 5--12$\times$ less orthogonal than at initialization (GPT-2-small: 0.297 vs 0.025). The fingerprint grows while orthogonality decays, ruling out isometry (Appendix~\ref{app:gpt2-details}).

To test whether gradient updates are themselves diagonal, we train 48-block residual MLPs (in\_dim$=$128, hidden$=$256, CIFAR-10, 50 epochs, 3 seeds) and measure gradient diagonality $g_{\mathrm{diag}} = |\mathrm{tr}(\nabla W_{\mathrm{out}} \nabla W_{\mathrm{in}})|/\|\cdot\|_F$ alongside the weight score $s(M)$. The gradient product stays flat (${\sim}0.15$), meaning individual updates are not diagonal. Yet the weight score climbs from 0.08 to ${\sim}4.0$ (Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism-triad}a). The diagonal structure emerges from many small correlated updates accumulating over training, not from any single diagonal gradient step.

Two interventions isolate gradient coupling as the cause. \emph{Necessity:} shuffling $\nabla W_{\mathrm{out}}$ across blocks before each optimizer step (block $i$'s $W_{\mathrm{out}}$ receives gradients from a random block $j$) breaks only the within-block pairing while preserving valid gradients. This cuts the fingerprint by 68\% ($s$: 3.90 $\to$ 1.24) at comparable accuracy (47\% vs 54\%) (Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism-triad}b). \emph{Sufficiency:} injecting synthetic updates $\Delta W_{\mathrm{in}} = -\varepsilon \cdot e_i$, $\Delta W_{\mathrm{out}} = -\varepsilon \cdot e_i^\top$ ($\varepsilon{=}0.01$) directly into weights for 3000 steps, with no loss function or backpropagation, builds the fingerprint from scratch ($s$: 0.08 $\to$ 7.57) (Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism-triad}c).

\subsection{Lineage Verification on Language Models}\label{sec:exp-verify}

To bridge the gap between synthetic MLPs and uncontrolled public checkpoints, we train 8 GPT-2-style transformers from scratch on TinyStories~\citep{eldan2023tinystories} with known ancestry. Each root (${\sim}$35M parameters: 6 layers, $d{=}384$, 50K samples, 3 epochs) is allocated to calibration ($n{=}2$), development ($n{=}3$), or test ($n{=}3$) splits. Per root we generate 7 descendants: 1 fine-tuned (1 epoch continued pretraining), 1 LoRA-merged (rank-8), 3 pruned (30/50/70\% sparsity), and 2 quantized (INT8/INT6). Quantization and LoRA preserve the signal almost perfectly ($\mathcal{L}{\geq}0.996$); fine-tuning and pruning degrade it but remain well above the null ($\mathcal{L}{\geq}0.855$). Our method contributes here not through AUROC differentiation, but through: (i) transfer to an actual autoregressive transformer architecture; (ii) a benchmark with known, root-disjoint ancestry; (iii) robustness across transformation types; (iv) block-level interpretable scores; and (v) data-free operation requiring no probe samples.

Table~\ref{tab:distill-quality} shows that models trained on the same architecture and corpus already exhibit substantial behavioral agreement: independent models achieve 77.6\% top-1 agreement. Distillation increases this modestly to 79.0\% (+1.4 points) and reduces KL divergence from 0.25 to 0.16. Yet both independently trained models and distilled students score near zero in lineage ($\mathcal{L} \approx 0.002$), correctly classified as unrelated.

This experiment validates our problem definition rather than differentiating our method: all methods correctly reject distilled students. The point is that behavioral similarity---whether from shared training setup or explicit imitation---does not imply weight ancestry. A verifier built solely on output comparison would conflate inheritance with imitation.

\subsection{Robustness Under Function-Preserving Reparameterization}\label{sec:exp-baselines}

Clean-benchmark performance measures proximity in a shared parameterization. A more stringent test asks whether the verifier captures ancestry independently of nuisance coordinate choices. We introduce \emph{function-preserving laundering}: transformations that leave the network's input--output map exactly unchanged while altering parameter coordinates.

Specifically, per-block permutation (P) permutes hidden units ($W_{\mathrm{in}} \leftarrow P W_{\mathrm{in}}$, $W_{\mathrm{out}} \leftarrow W_{\mathrm{out}} P^\top$), and reciprocal rescaling (D) scales them ($W_{\mathrm{in}} \leftarrow D W_{\mathrm{in}}$, $W_{\mathrm{out}} \leftarrow W_{\mathrm{out}} D^{-1}$). These transformations preserve the branch product $M = W_{\mathrm{out}} W_{\mathrm{in}}$ exactly ($P^\top P = I$, $D^{-1} D = I$), so our signature is invariant by construction. Raw-weight baselines have no such guarantee.

\paragraph{MLP laundering benchmark.}
We evaluate on a 52-pair MLP benchmark (depth-16, in\_dim=16, hidden=48) with six laundering variants: none, P, D-mild (log-uniform $[0.5,2]$), D-strong (log-uniform $[0.1,10]$), PD, and PDFT (P+D then 5 epochs fine-tuning). All variants pass a hard function-preservation gate ($\max|\Delta f| < 10^{-4}$).

Table~\ref{tab:laundering} shows the results. Our method maintains AUROC$=$1.000 across all variants---the measured signature deviation is $|\Delta\mathcal{L}| \approx 10^{-8}$, confirming algebraic invariance. Raw-weight baselines collapse: aligned Frobenius drops to 0.50 under P alone and to 0.00 under strong D; singular-value distance survives P (spectrum is permutation-invariant) but fails D; weight cosine partially survives both but degrades to 0.80 under PD. A scale-aware Re-Basin baseline~\citep{ainsworth2023git}, which explicitly solves a per-block permutation and scale optimization to undo the laundering, also achieves 1.000 on all variants. The distinction is cost: our signature is invariant with zero alignment work, while Re-Basin must solve $L$ Hungarian assignments plus $L$ least-squares fits for every pair.

\paragraph{GPT-2 hidden-unit permutation.}
We extend the laundering analysis to our GPT-2-style language model benchmark (Section~\ref{sec:exp-verify}) to test permutation invariance at scale. Hidden-unit permutation applies $W_{\mathrm{fc}} \leftarrow P W_{\mathrm{fc}}$, $b_{\mathrm{fc}} \leftarrow P b_{\mathrm{fc}}$, $W_{\mathrm{proj}} \leftarrow W_{\mathrm{proj}} P^\top$ per block, which is exact for GELU (unlike rescaling, which requires positive homogeneity). We test four conditions: NONE (no permutation), P-SUSPECT (permute suspect only), P-BOTH (different random permutations on both reference and suspect), and P+FT (permute suspect then fine-tune for 1000 steps). Each permutation condition uses 5 random seeds.

Table~\ref{tab:gpt2-laundering} reports AUROC across conditions. All methods maintain AUROC$=$1.0 (except SVD at 0.76), but the \emph{score magnitudes} reveal the key difference. Why does AUROC remain 1.0 despite the score collapse? Permutation affects descendants and non-descendants similarly, preserving ranking while shrinking the margin by ${\sim}30\times$ (from 0.976 to 0.033). In noisy real-world settings, such thin margins would fail. Our method maintains the original wide margin throughout.

This case study demonstrates real-world plausibility but has limited calibration power: only one confirmed independent model (OpenLLaMA) exists for the LLaMA-2 architecture. We report the ${\sim}1000\times$ score gap descriptively rather than as a calibrated hypothesis test. Practical deployment at specific confidence levels would require collecting additional null models of matched architecture.
