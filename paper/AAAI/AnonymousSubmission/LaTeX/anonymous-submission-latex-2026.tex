\begin{abstract}
Open-weight checkpoints are routinely fine-tuned, quantized, pruned, merged, and redistributed without reliable lineage metadata. Existing provenance tools are brittle: hashes fail after any weight change, metadata requires trust, and watermarks must be inserted before release. \textbf{We study a post-hoc white-box weight-lineage verification: given two checkpoints with compatible residual architecture, do they share weight-level lineage after common checkpoint transformations?} This is distinct from behavioral similarity: a distilled student imitates its teacher's outputs, but its weights are trained from scratch and share no lineage.

We show that residual-network training induces an intrinsic fingerprint: correctly paired residual-branch products develop a diagonal-dominant fingerprint, measured by a trace/Frobenius score, that is absent at initialization and in non-residual controls. We use this signal to align blocks between checkpoints and compute a model-level lineage score from centered branch signatures. Across 12 architectures spanning language, vision, and speech (GPT-2 to LLaMA-2, ViT, Whisper, ResNets), the fingerprint recovers correct projection pairings with 91--100\% accuracy, and the lineage score achieves complete separation: fine-tuned, pruned, quantized, and LoRA-merged checkpoints score high, while independently trained models and distilled students remain low. Unlike activation-based CKA and IPGuard, which track functional similarity, this provides a passive, retroactive provenance signal for white-box residual checkpoints.
\end{abstract}

The open-weight ecosystem has transformed how neural networks are distributed and reused. When Meta released LLaMA~\citep{touvron2023llama}, the community produced instruction-tuned variants within weeks. Platforms like Hugging Face now host hundreds of thousands of model checkpoints~\citep{huggingface2024}, many of which are fine-tuned~\citep{devlin2019bert,ouyang2022rlhf}, quantized~\citep{han2016deep,dettmers2022llmint8}, pruned~\citep{han2015deep}, or merged~\citep{wortsman2022model,ilharco2023editing}. This creates a \emph{model supply chain} where downstream checkpoints inherit weights from upstream models, but the lineage is often undocumented or intentionally obscured. Provenance of AI-generated content is now treated as a first-class problem: Google's SynthID watermarks text and images at generation time~\citep{dathathri2024synthid}, yet no analogous mechanism exists for the models themselves. Model reuse is easy; reliable model provenance is not.

Existing provenance mechanisms fall short (Table~\ref{tab:comparison}). Cryptographic hashes break after any weight modification. Metadata and model cards~\citep{mitchell2019model} require honest distributors and preserved records; they can be falsified or lost. Watermarks~\citep{uchida2017embedding,adi2018turning} must be inserted before release; they cannot be applied retroactively to already-deployed checkpoints, and no surveyed scheme proved robust against fine-tuning or pruning~\citep{lukas2022sok}. Behavioral tests that compare model outputs cannot distinguish weight inheritance from imitation: two models can behave similarly without sharing weights, especially after distillation~\citep{hinton2015distilling}. Activation-based similarity measures like CKA~\citep{kornblith2019similarity} and decision-boundary fingerprints like IPGuard~\citep{cao2021ipguard} inherit this limitation. We need a passive, post-hoc signal that reads provenance from the weights themselves.

We ask: given a reference checkpoint and a suspect checkpoint with the same residual architecture (e.g., both LLaMA-2-7B derivatives), can we determine whether the suspect inherited weights from the reference, even after common transformations like fine-tuning, quantization, or pruning? This is distinct from duplicate detection (which hashes solve until the first modification), behavioral similarity (which conflates imitation with inheritance), and watermark verification (which requires forethought). The question is whether shared weight ancestry leaves detectable structure after modification.

The answer is yes. We find that training itself leaves a characteristic fingerprint in the weights of residual networks. In these architectures~\citep{he2016deep}, each block adds a learned transformation to its input via a skip connection (Figure~\ref{fig:hero}a). During training, the input and output projections of each block align so that their product $W_{\mathrm{out}}W_{\mathrm{in}}$ develops diagonal-dominant structure that is absent at random initialization and does not appear in networks without skip connections (Figure~\ref{fig:hero}b). This structure emerges from the optimization process: it is learned, not an initialization artifact (Section~\ref{sec:diagonal-dominance}).

We exploit this fingerprint for provenance verification. Each residual block yields a signature: its branch product scored for diagonal concentration via a trace/Frobenius ratio (Equation~\ref{eq:dd-score}). Correctly paired blocks from the same trained model score high, while mismatched or unrelated blocks do not. The extraction is architecture-aware, respecting the specific structure of Transformer MLPs, attention paths, and ResNet bottleneck blocks (Table~\ref{tab:factorization}). To compare two checkpoints, we align their blocks using these signatures, aggregate the pairwise similarities into a model-level lineage score, and threshold it against a null distribution built from unrelated models (Equation~\ref{eq:lineage-score}).

The fingerprint is not specific to one model family. We observe it in public checkpoints across language, vision, and speech (GPT-2, BERT, LLaMA-2, Mistral, Qwen2.5, DeepSeek-R1, ViT, Whisper, and ResNets) where it recovers block correspondences with over 91\% accuracy (Table~\ref{tab:transformer-family}). Turning to the verification task itself, we construct controlled lineage benchmarks on MLPs and ResNet-18: models with genuine weight ancestry (produced by fine-tuning, weight noise, pruning, quantization, or LoRA merging) are cleanly separated from those without (independent training runs and distilled students), with AUROC = 1.000. The distillation case is instructive: a student can closely replicate its teacher's outputs, yet our score correctly labels it unrelated, because its weights carry no trace of the teacher's training (Figure~\ref{fig:hero}c). Finally, on the public LLaMA-2 family, the method flags three real fine-tuned descendants (chat, Vicuna, CodeLlama) as related while rejecting four independently trained checkpoints, among them OpenLLaMA, which shares the architecture but inherits no weights.

Our contributions:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
\item \textbf{Diagonal-dominant fingerprint.} We establish that gradient-based training drives the input and output projections of residual blocks toward mutual alignment, concentrating their product along the diagonal, a property absent at random initialization and never acquired by skip-free architectures.
\item \textbf{White-box verification method.} We convert this signature into a practical test: per-block scores are matched across two checkpoints, aggregated into a single lineage statistic, and judged against a null distribution, requiring no watermark, no metadata, and no cooperation from the model's distributor.
\item \textbf{Broad empirical validation.} We evaluate on 280+ checkpoint pairs covering 12 architectures and 5 model families, from synthetic benchmarks with known ground truth to real-world derivatives of LLaMA-2, obtaining 91--100\% block correspondence and AUROC$=$1.000.
\item \textbf{Robustness and specificity.} The fingerprint persists through fine-tuning, quantization, pruning, and LoRA merging, yet vanishes for distilled students and independently trained models of identical architecture, a distinction beyond the reach of behavioral measures such as CKA and IPGuard.
\end{itemize}

This section formalizes the lineage verification problem and positions it against prior provenance mechanisms.

\subsection{White-Box Model Lineage Verification}

\textbf{Scope.} A verifier is given two checkpoints $A$ and $B$ and must determine whether they share weight-level lineage. We assume white-box access to both weight sets and compatible residual architectures (same layer count $L$ and hidden dimension $d$); cross-architecture comparisons are out of scope. Metadata may be missing or adversarial. The verifier operates on weights alone: no training data, activations, or forward passes are available.

\textbf{Relatedness.} Checkpoints are \emph{related} if they share a common weight ancestor: either one derives from the other, or both derive from a shared parent, through a chain of transformations $\theta' = T(\theta, D)$, where $T$ may include continued optimization (fine-tuning, RLHF), compression (pruning, quantization), or adapter merging (LoRA), and $D$ is optional additional data. Checkpoints are \emph{unrelated} if their weights were initialized independently, even when they share the same architecture, training data, task, or output behavior. Transformations with multiple parents, such as linear merges of two checkpoints, induce partial lineage between the suspect and each parent; we quantify this regime in Appendix~\ref{app:harder-bench}.

The verifier solves lineage verification: do $A$ and $B$ share weight ancestry? It returns one of three verdicts: \textsc{Related}, \textsc{Unrelated}, or \textsc{Inconclusive} (insufficient evidence either way). The lineage score is symmetric, $\mathcal{L}(A,B) = \mathcal{L}(B,A)$: the protocol establishes whether two checkpoints share ancestry, not which is the ancestor; directional attribution requires metadata or additional candidates (Appendix~\ref{app:verification}). As a by-product, the verification pipeline recovers block-level correspondences between the two checkpoints (Section~\ref{sec:pairing}), which serve as a diagnostic when the verdict is \textsc{Related}.

\textbf{Lineage follows initialization, not behavior.} Under this definition, what determines lineage is where the weights came from, not what the model does. A distilled student trained from freshly initialized weights is unrelated to its teacher, no matter how closely it imitates the teacher's outputs; model-extraction copies that replicate a model's function through query access~\citep{tramer2016stealing} are likewise unrelated. Conversely, a student initialized from its teacher's weights is weight-related regardless of the distillation objective: initialization, not training procedure, determines lineage. The method is thus a forensic primitive for weight-level provenance, complementing cryptographic signing, metadata, and watermarking where those were never applied; it is not a legal ownership proof.

\subsection{Related Work}

We organize prior work by the signal each method measures; Table~\ref{tab:comparison} summarizes the comparison.

\textbf{Cryptographic and metadata provenance.} Cryptographic hashes verify exact byte-level identity but fail after any weight modification: the avalanche property ensures that fine-tuning, pruning, or even floating-point rounding produces an entirely different digest. Model cards~\citep{mitchell2019model} and experiment-tracking systems document provenance but require honest distributors and preserved records; they can be falsified or lost when checkpoints are redistributed outside controlled environments. Proof-of-learning protocols~\citep{jia2021proofoflearning} verify that a claimed party performed the training, but require retained training transcripts: forethought that already-released checkpoints lack.

\textbf{Embedded ownership signals.} Parameter watermarks~\citep{uchida2017embedding} and backdoor watermarks~\citep{adi2018turning} embed deliberate signals during training for later extraction. \citet{lukas2022sok} found no surveyed scheme reliably robust against fine-tuning and pruning within realistic compute budgets, and all require proactive insertion: unwatermarked legacy checkpoints cannot be verified retroactively. Output watermarks like SynthID mark AI-generated content, not weights: a different problem.

\textbf{Behavioral and decision-boundary fingerprints.} IPGuard~\citep{cao2021ipguard} extracts inputs near the classification boundary as fingerprints; adversarial frontier stitching~\citep{lemerrer2020adversarial} uses adversarial examples to mark decision surfaces. These measure functional similarity and cannot distinguish weight inheritance from imitation: a distilled student may share decision surfaces while having entirely independent weights.

\textbf{Representation similarity.} CKA~\citep{kornblith2019similarity} and SVCCA~\citep{raghu2017svcca} compare learned representations using activation patterns on a probe dataset. They may regard a successful distilled student as similar: reasonable for their objective (representation alignment) but not ours (weight ancestry). They also require data and forward passes, whereas our method operates on weights alone.

\textbf{Weight-space provenance.} Generic parameter-space similarity (weight cosine, Frobenius distance, permutation-aligned matching~\citep{ainsworth2023git}) can detect derived checkpoints when the suspect remains close to the reference in parameter space. But these are uncalibrated global scalars: they localize nothing, offer no account of why proximity implies lineage or when it stops doing so, and their margins are not grounded in any property of training. A recent line builds purpose-built provenance on weights, but each requires something beyond the two checkpoints: HuRef~\citep{zeng2025huref} relies on convergence of LLM parameter directions after pretraining; REEF~\citep{zhang2024reef} and neural lineage detection~\citep{yu2024neurallineage} need probe data, activations, or gradients; MoTHer~\citep{horwitz2025mother} recovers directed model trees but requires a collection of checkpoints rather than a single pair. None of these can say which weights were inherited. Our fingerprint is read block by block from trained residual structure: it is data-free, compares a single pair of checkpoints against a calibrated null, and reveals not only whether a suspect inherited weights but which blocks it inherited and how many.


\section{Training-Induced Diagonal Dominance in Residual Branch Products}\label{sec:diagonal-dominance}

A residual block computes $x_{\ell+1} = x_\ell + F_\ell(x_\ell)$, where $F_\ell$ is the residual branch. For a branch with $K$ linear layers $W_1, \ldots, W_K$ interleaved with nonlinearities, we define the \emph{residual branch product} by composing the linear factors and dropping the nonlinearities between them:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:branch-product}
M_\ell = W_K W_{K-1} \cdots W_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}
\end{equation}
Individual factors $W_k$ are rectangular and do not share an input--output basis; their composed product maps the residual stream back into its own coordinate space, so its diagonal entries compare like with like. The exact factorization is architecture-dependent (Table~\ref{tab:factorization}).

\subsection{Why Residual Training Induces Diagonal Dominance}\label{sec:mechanism}

Under the Frobenius inner product $\langle A, B \rangle = \mathrm{tr}(A^\top B)$, the identity matrix and the traceless matrices span orthogonal subspaces of $\mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$. Any branch product therefore splits into a component along the identity, with coefficient $\langle M_\ell, I \rangle / \langle I, I \rangle = \mathrm{tr}(M_\ell)/d$, and an orthogonal traceless remainder:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:decomp}
M_\ell = -\varepsilon_\ell I + E_\ell
\end{equation}
where $\varepsilon_\ell = -\mathrm{tr}(M_\ell)/d$ and $\mathrm{tr}(E_\ell) = 0$.
The sign convention absorbs the empirical regularity that traces of trained branches are predominantly negative (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}), making $\varepsilon_\ell$ typically positive; nothing downstream depends on the sign, since the score below uses the trace magnitude. The decomposition itself is a coordinate choice, available for any matrix.

We measure this concentration with the \emph{diagonal-dominance score}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dd-score}
s(M) = \frac{|\mathrm{tr}(M)|}{\|M\|_F},
\end{equation}
the fraction of the matrix's energy lying along the identity direction, up to a factor of $\sqrt{d}$. The finding is that training moves energy into the identity component: in trained residual networks, correctly paired branch products score far above unpaired or untrained ones, a concentration absent at initialization and never developed without skip connections (Figure~\ref{fig:hero}b).

The skip connection explains why. It places the learned branch in an identity-centered parameterization: training optimizes a correction around an explicit identity path rather than learning the full transformation from scratch. For stable gradient flow, the block Jacobian $J = I + J_F$ should remain near-orthogonal throughout training, a condition known as dynamical isometry~\citep{pennington2017resurrecting}. If $J \approx I$, then $J_F \approx 0$; for small but nonzero learned corrections, the linear component of $J_F$ tends toward $-\varepsilon I$ with small $\varepsilon$. Because $W_{\mathrm{in}}$ and $W_{\mathrm{out}}$ receive coupled gradients through the branch, this pressure aligns their product with coordinate-preserving directions. Non-residual networks lack this constraint: without the skip connection, there is no functional pressure toward identity, and nothing couples $W_{\mathrm{in}}$ with $W_{\mathrm{out}}$ more than with any other weight matrix.

This is a mechanistic hypothesis, not a theorem, but each of its predictions is testable. It predicts no signal at initialization under any scheme: an ablation over seven initializations finds chance-level pairing before training, including orthogonal initialization, which satisfies dynamical isometry by construction yet has learned nothing~\citep{saxe2014exact} (Appendix~\ref{app:init-ablation}). It predicts no signal without the skip connection: a PlainNet trained to comparable loss develops none (Appendix~\ref{app:plainnet}).

\subsection{Margin Analysis}\label{sec:margin}

For the score to separate correct pairings from incorrect ones, two things must hold: correctly paired products must score high, and mismatched products must score low. We treat each in turn.

By the orthogonality of decomposition~\eqref{eq:decomp}, $\|M_\ell\|_F^2 = \varepsilon_\ell^2 d + \|E_\ell\|_F^2$: the branch product's energy splits exactly between its identity component and its traceless remainder. The score therefore depends on $M$ only through the ratio of the two:

\begin{proposition}[Correct-pair score]\label{prop:margin}
Let $M = -\varepsilon I + E$ with $\mathrm{tr}(E) = 0$ and $\varepsilon \neq 0$. Then:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:margin-formula}
s(M) = \frac{\sqrt{d}}{\sqrt{1 + \|E\|_F^2 / (\varepsilon^2 d)}} \leq \sqrt{d}
\end{equation}
with equality if and only if $E = 0$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$|\mathrm{tr}(M)| = |\varepsilon| d$ and $\|M\|_F^2 = \varepsilon^2 d + \|E\|_F^2$ by orthogonality. Substitute into (3).
\end{proof}

The score is $\sqrt{d}$ discounted by how much the learned residue $E$ outweighs the identity component: geometrically, $s(M)/\sqrt{d}$ is the cosine of the angle between $M$ and the identity. A correct pair scores high exactly to the extent that training concentrated its product along the diagonal.

A mismatched product has no such concentration. For $W_{\mathrm{out}}$ and $W_{\mathrm{in}}$ drawn from different blocks, the product has no preferred alignment with the identity: its trace is a sum of $d$ weakly correlated terms of comparable size to the other $d^2$ entries, giving

\begin{corollary}[Mismatched baseline]\label{cor:baseline}
For products of projections from independent blocks, $\mathbb{E}[s] \approx 1/\sqrt{d}$.
\end{corollary}

The margin between the two regimes therefore scales as $d$: wider networks separate correct from incorrect pairings more cleanly, not less. Empirically the correct-pair score grows as $d^{0.87}$ (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}), faster than the $\sqrt{d}$ envelope's rate, consistent with $\|E\|_F/(\varepsilon\sqrt{d})$ shrinking as $d$ grows, while always remaining below the $\sqrt{d}$ bound. The proposition bounds the score; it does not fix its growth rate.

\section{From Diagonal-Dominance Fingerprints to Lineage Verification}\label{sec:verification}

Diagonal dominance identifies correctly paired trained residual branches, but it does not establish lineage: unrelated trained models both exhibit it. The decomposition~\eqref{eq:decomp} supplies the division of labor. The identity component is generic; its strength certifies that a branch carries trained residual structure, and Section~\ref{sec:exp-arch} uses it to recover projection pairings within a model. But precisely because it is shared across trained models, it says nothing about ancestry. The traceless component $E_\ell$ is block- and checkpoint-specific: it survives weight-preserving transformations but not reinitialization. Verification therefore runs on the traceless component, with diagonal dominance serving as a gate on which branches are trustworthy. This section builds the protocol in three steps: signatures, alignment, decision.

\subsection{Centered Residual Signatures and Block Alignment}\label{sec:pairing}

For each branch product $M_\ell$ we retain only the traceless remainder of decomposition~\eqref{eq:decomp}, normalized to a unit vector:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:centered-fingerprint}
R_\ell = M_\ell - \tfrac{\mathrm{tr}(M_\ell)}{d} \cdot I, \quad
\phi_\ell = \tfrac{\mathrm{vec}(R_\ell)}{\|\mathrm{vec}(R_\ell)\|_2}
\end{equation}
$R_\ell$ is exactly the $E_\ell$ of~\eqref{eq:decomp}; the signature $\phi_\ell$ carries the block- and checkpoint-specific geometry that weight-preserving transformations inherit and independent training cannot reproduce. Because $M_\ell$ is exactly invariant under the two function-preserving reparameterizations of a residual branch (hidden permutation: $W_{\mathrm{in}} \leftarrow P W_{\mathrm{in}}$, $W_{\mathrm{out}} \leftarrow W_{\mathrm{out}} P^\top$; reciprocal rescaling: $W_{\mathrm{in}} \leftarrow c W_{\mathrm{in}}$, $W_{\mathrm{out}} \leftarrow W_{\mathrm{out}}/c$), so is every signature built on it: re-basing hidden units cannot evade the comparison (Section~\ref{sec:boundaries}).

Given reference $A$ and suspect $B$, each with $L$ blocks, we form the signature-similarity matrix $G_{ij} = \langle \phi^A_i, \phi^B_j \rangle$, gated so that branches with weak diagonal dominance in either checkpoint contribute conservatively (Appendix~\ref{app:verification}), and recover the block correspondence as the assignment maximizing total similarity,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hungarian}
\pi^* = \arg\max_{\pi \in \mathcal{P}_L} \sum_{i} G_{i,\pi(i)},
\end{equation}
via the Hungarian algorithm~\citep{kuhn1955hungarian}. For derived checkpoints that preserve block ordering, $\pi^*$ should be the identity permutation, and recovering it without metadata is evidence that the signatures track block identity rather than position; the Hungarian step matters when ordering cannot be trusted, and its output doubles as a diagnostic: when the verdict is \textsc{Related}, the correspondence matrix shows which blocks were inherited (Appendix~\ref{app:experiments}). Computing the branch products costs $O(Ld^2 h)$, where $h$ is the branch's inner width; the similarity matrix adds $O(L^2 d^2)$ and the assignment a negligible $O(L^3)$.

\subsection{Lineage Score and Verification Test}\label{sec:lineage-score}

The lineage score averages signature similarity over aligned branches:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lineage-score}
\mathcal{L}(A, B) = \frac{1}{L} \sum_{\ell=1}^{L} \langle \phi^A_\ell, \phi^B_{\pi^*(\ell)} \rangle
\end{equation}
Each term is a cosine, so $\mathcal{L} \in [-1,1]$ with $\mathcal{L}(A,A) = 1$. The score is symmetric, $\mathcal{L}(A,B) = \mathcal{L}(B,A)$: exchanging the roles of $A$ and $B$ transposes $G$, and the optimal assignment value is invariant under transposition, provided the gate is defined over both checkpoints jointly (Appendix~\ref{app:verification}). Related checkpoints should score near $1$ and unrelated ones near $0$, since independently initialized weights produce uncorrelated $E_\ell$; Section~\ref{sec:experiments} tests both predictions.

To map the score onto the verdicts of Section~\ref{sec:background}, we calibrate against a null distribution: with $\mathcal{N}_A = \{\mathcal{L}(A, U_j)\}$ the scores between $A$ and independently trained models $U_j$, and $(\mu_0, \sigma_0)$ its mean and standard deviation,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:zscore}
Z(A,B) = \frac{\mathcal{L}(A,B) - \mu_0}{\sigma_0}
\end{equation}
The protocol returns
\begin{equation}
V(A,B) = \begin{cases}
\textsc{Related} & \text{if } Z > \tau_{\text{upper}} \\
\textsc{Unrelated} & \text{if } Z < \tau_{\text{lower}} \\
\textsc{Inconclusive} & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
with symmetric thresholds $\tau_{\text{upper}} = 3.0$, $\tau_{\text{lower}} = -3.0$, giving a false-positive rate below $0.13\%$ under a Gaussian null. Compatibility (same layer count $L$ and hidden dimension $d$, per the setting of Section~\ref{sec:background}) is a precondition checked before any scoring; the protocol returns \textsc{Incompatible} when it fails, an enforcement of the setting rather than a fourth verdict. The verifier needs weights alone, and $O(L^2 + d^2)$ working memory beyond the checkpoints themselves.

\section{Experiments}\label{sec:experiments}

Our experiments validate the claim ladder of Sections~\ref{sec:diagonal-dominance}--\ref{sec:verification} in order. Section~\ref{sec:exp-exists} tests the three mechanistic predictions of Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}: the fingerprint is absent at initialization under every scheme tested, never develops without skip connections, and emerges within the first few epochs of residual training. Section~\ref{sec:exp-arch} tests the extraction machinery of Section~\ref{sec:pairing} and the margin analysis of Proposition~\ref{prop:margin}, showing that architecture-aware branch products recover block correspondences across 12 architectures spanning language, vision, and speech, with a correct-pair score that grows as $d^{0.87}$ against the $\sqrt{d}$ envelope. Section~\ref{sec:exp-verify} tests the full verification protocol of Section~\ref{sec:lineage-score} at three levels of realism: controlled MLP benchmarks with known ground truth, a CIFAR-10 ResNet-18 benchmark, and real fine-tuned derivatives of LLaMA-2-7B scored against a cross-model null. Section~\ref{sec:exp-baselines} positions the method against weight-space, activation-based, and decision-boundary baselines. Extended ablations are in Appendices~\ref{app:experiments} and~\ref{app:architectures}.

\subsection{The Fingerprint Exists and Is Learned}\label{sec:exp-exists}

Section~\ref{sec:mechanism} predicts that diagonal dominance is learned, not an initialization artifact, and requires the skip connection. We test each prediction.

\textbf{Emergence during training.} On a 48-block residual network, block-pairing accuracy is at the chance baseline ($6.25\%$) at epoch 0 and reaches $100\%$ by epoch 5, well before loss plateaus. By epoch 300, the mean correct-pair score exceeds the mean incorrect-pair score by $21\times$ (Appendix~\ref{app:experiments}). The fingerprint develops rapidly in early training.

\textbf{The skip connection is necessary.} We train a PlainNet---architecturally identical to ResNet except that skip connections are removed---to comparable evaluation loss (1.56 vs 1.35). On a 24-block network (chance ${\approx}4.2\%$), ResNet achieves $100\%$ pair accuracy with $100\%$ of correctly paired products exhibiting negative trace; PlainNet achieves only $3\%$ accuracy (at chance) with $47\%$ negative-trace incidence and AUC 0.51 (Appendix~\ref{app:plainnet}). The skip connection is structurally necessary.

\textbf{Not an initialization artifact.} An ablation over seven initialization schemes---Kaiming uniform/normal, Xavier uniform/normal, Gaussian, uniform, and orthogonal---finds $\leq 2.1\%$ pair accuracy before training across all schemes (Appendix~\ref{app:init-ablation}). After training, six schemes reach 94--100\% accuracy. Orthogonal initialization is the critical case: it provides dynamical isometry at initialization by construction~\citep{saxe2014exact}, satisfying the near-identity Jacobian condition of Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}, yet shows \emph{zero} correct pairs before training. This rules out the alternative explanation that near-orthogonal Jacobians alone create the fingerprint; what matters is isometry under training pressure. The seventh case, $\sigma{=}0.02$ Gaussian, fails (13\% trained) because blocks collapse to near-zero contribution, removing the Jacobian constraint.

\textbf{Factorization must match the architecture.} The branch product carries the signal only when it composes every linear factor of one branch. For ResNet Bottleneck blocks the two-factor product $W_3 W_1$ pairs at chance, while the full $W_3 W_2 W_1$ recovers 91--100\% (Appendix~\ref{app:architectures}); for SwiGLU MLPs, scoring the gate and up paths separately loses accuracy that the joint product $W_{\mathrm{down}} W_{\mathrm{up}}$ restores. Scale works for the verifier.

\textbf{The contribution claim.} We do not claim that diagonal dominance beats weight cosine on aggregate AUROC. The contribution is that the residual-product signature (i) yields per-block correspondence as a by-product, (ii) admits the closed-form margin analysis of Proposition~\ref{prop:margin}, and (iii) is interpretable in terms of trained residual geometry. On the real 7B weights of Section~\ref{sec:exp-verify}, the $1850\times$-vs-$6\times$ margin gap matters: aligned Frobenius and SV-distance underperform substantially on this real-model set (AUROC$=$0.667, above chance but far from the saturation reached on synthetic benchmarks).

\textbf{The harder regime.} On layer grafts and linear merges (Appendix~\ref{app:harder-bench}, 40 pairs), diagonal dominance, aligned Frobenius, and weight cosine remain at or near AUROC$=$1.000. Singular-value distance underperforms on merges ($0.448$); CKA drops to ${\approx}0.80$; IPGuard achieves AUROC$=$0.05 on grafts---below chance, indicating systematic misclassification where its decision-boundary assumptions break down. The diagonal-dominance score retains Spearman $\rho \approx 0.96$ with the reference fraction, supporting the interpretability claim: it tracks partial overlap monotonically, not merely crossing a threshold.

\section{Robustness and Boundary Cases}\label{sec:boundaries}

Section~\ref{sec:experiments} established that the protocol detects lineage. This section maps its edges under three pressures: benign post-training modification (Section~\ref{sec:survival}), deliberate evasion (Section~\ref{sec:adversarial}), and definitional boundaries where the method correctly declines or genuinely fails (Section~\ref{sec:failure-modes}).

\subsection{Survival Under Post-Training Modification}\label{sec:survival}

The question is not whether the fingerprint detects lineage---Section~\ref{sec:exp-verify} established that---but what happens as transformation intensity increases. Table~\ref{tab:survival} shows survival across five transformation families: fine-tuning (same/different target), Gaussian noise ($\sigma_{\mathrm{rel}} \le 15\%$), magnitude pruning (10--85\%), quantization (16--256 levels), and LoRA adapter merging.

Fine-tuning, quantization, and noise preserve the signal with mean $\mathcal{L} = 0.99$ and minimum $0.97$. Fine-tuning to a different target degrades to mean $0.94$ (min $0.84$). Pruning shows the steepest decline: from $\mathcal{L} = 0.99$ at 10\% sparsity to $0.58$ at 85\% sparsity. LoRA-merged checkpoints score $\mathcal{L} \in [0.97, 0.99]$ ($\sim$formal detection counts pending). CodeLlama ($\mathcal{L}=0.336$), the real-world far endpoint of the fine-tuning axis after heavy code-specialized continued pretraining, remains ${\sim}700\times$ above the unrelated null.

The thesis: fingerprint degradation correlates with utility loss ($\rho = -0.83$; Figure~\ref{fig:lineage-three-panel}b). No benign path removes the signal without damaging the model. The two are structurally coupled through the Jacobian constraint of Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}.

\subsection{Adversarial Suppression and Function-Preserving Symmetries}\label{sec:adversarial}

\textbf{Function-preserving symmetries.} Per-block permutation ($W_{\mathrm{in}} \leftarrow P W_{\mathrm{in}}$, $W_{\mathrm{out}} \leftarrow W_{\mathrm{out}} P^\top$) and reciprocal rescaling ($W_{\mathrm{in}} \leftarrow c W_{\mathrm{in}}$, $W_{\mathrm{out}} \leftarrow W_{\mathrm{out}}/c$) leave the branch product $M = W_{\mathrm{out}} W_{\mathrm{in}}$ exactly unchanged, so $\mathcal{L} = 1.0$ to numerical precision; orthogonal rotations also preserve $M$ but break the network, since ReLU does not commute with rotation. Re-basing hidden units therefore buys an adversary nothing: the signature is built on the one object these reparameterizations cannot move.

\textbf{Gradient-based suppression.} A stronger adversary optimizes a perturbation $\Delta$ directly against the score while constraining function:
\begin{equation}
\min_{\Delta} \;\mathcal{L}(A, A + \Delta) \;+\; \lambda \cdot \|f_{A+\Delta}(X) - f_A(X)\|_2^2
\end{equation}
Sweeping $\lambda$ traces a Pareto frontier (Appendix~\ref{app:attacks}). With $\lambda \ge 1$ the attack cannot push $\mathcal{L}$ below $0.91$; reaching the unrelated range ($\mathcal{L} \approx 0.08$) costs at least 12\% eval-loss degradation; removing the constraint destroys the model (a $57\times$ loss increase). An attacker with white-box knowledge of the score faces the same trade that Section~\ref{sec:survival} measured for benign transformations: the signal does not come off without taking function with it.

\textbf{Untested attack surfaces.} We have evaluated one adaptive attack; two remain open. Re-alignment toward a third model in the style of Git Re-Basin could attempt to rotate the $E$ matrices off their checkpoint-specific directions, and fine-tuning with an explicit decorrelation penalty on $\langle E_{\mathrm{suspect}}, E_{\mathrm{reference}}\rangle$ is a fingerprint-aware strengthening of the attack above; we leave both to future work. Warm-start distillation---a student initialized from teacher weights, then distilled---is not an evasion candidate: under the definition of Section~\ref{sec:background} it is \textsc{Related}, and it functions as a positive control on the distillation boundary rather than an attack on it.

\subsection{Boundary Cases and Failure Modes}\label{sec:failure-modes}

Some conditions the method handles correctly but uninformatively; others it cannot handle at all. We separate the two.

\textbf{Correct boundary behaviors.} Distillation is the most important. Students trained from fresh weights are \textsc{Unrelated} by definition, and the verdicts agree: same-architecture distilled students score $\mathcal{L} = 0.086$ on the controlled benchmark, and the DeepSeek distill sits at the $10^{-4}$ cross-model null on the real test (Sections~\ref{sec:exp-verify}--\ref{sec:exp-baselines}). The gap between those two magnitudes is itself informative---same-architecture distillation on shared data retains a trace of accidental correlation that cross-family comparison lacks---and both fall well below threshold. Symmetry is the second boundary: $\mathcal{L}(A,B) = \mathcal{L}(B,A)$ by construction, so the protocol cannot name the ancestor; direction requires metadata or a third candidate, as in the branching tree of Section~\ref{sec:exp-verify}. Partial lineage is the third: grafts and merges form a graded regime in which $\mathcal{L}$ tracks the fraction of inherited blocks with Spearman $\rho \approx 0.96$ (Appendix~\ref{app:harder-bench})---partial overlap is measured, not misclassified.

\textbf{Genuine failures} (Table~\ref{tab:limitations}). The protocol returns \textsc{Incompatible} when depth or width differ, and there is no signal to read in non-residual networks (the PlainNet of Section~\ref{sec:exp-exists}) or when blocks collapse to zero contribution (the $\sigma = 0.02$ initialization). Utility-destroying perturbations such as 2-bit quantization take the fingerprint down with the model, and no verdict is possible about a common ancestor that neither party can produce.

\textbf{The \textsc{Inconclusive} verdict.} Across all 280+ tested pairs, every score fell cleanly above $\tau_{\mathrm{upper}}$ or below $\tau_{\mathrm{lower}}$; no verdict was \textsc{Inconclusive}. The band exists for deployments our benchmarks do not cover---nulls built from few independents, or suspects near the graft and merge midpoints of Appendix~\ref{app:harder-bench}---where scores need not separate as sharply as they do here.

\section{Discussion}

\textbf{Scope.} The method requires white-box access and residual architecture. Non-residual networks lack the identity-target constraint that creates the fingerprint.

\textbf{Positioning.} This complements watermarking: watermarks require forethought; diagonal dominance offers passive, retroactive verification for checkpoints never watermarked.

\textbf{Validation scope.} Lineage AUROC is reported on the synthetic MLP zoo and CIFAR-10 ResNet-18, with real-architecture evidence from pairing on Transformer families and a 7-pair LLaMA-2 test. The remaining gap is tree breadth: all related checkpoints derive from a single base. Extending to additional checkpoint trees and larger negative sets is straightforward engineering. Detailed limitations are in Appendix~\ref{app:limitations}.

\section{Conclusion}

Training leaves traces. Residual-network optimization couples each block's input and output projections so that their product becomes diagonal-dominant ($W_{\mathrm{out}} W_{\mathrm{in}} \approx -\varepsilon I + E$), with a theoretical $\sqrt{d}$ upper bound and an empirical $d^{0.87}$ growth observed from GPT-2 through LLaMA-2-13B and Mixtral-8x7B. Across 12 architectures spanning 5 families, the fingerprint recovers 91--100\% of block correspondences and achieves AUROC$=$1.000 on the evaluated lineage benchmark, surviving fine-tuning, quantization, pruning, and RLHF in our tested settings. Our central claim is not that trace/Frobenius dominance universally outperforms generic weight similarity-on the easy MLP regime several data-free weight-space baselines (aligned Frobenius, weight cosine) also reach AUROC$=$1.000-but that trained residual products expose an interpretable, block-localized, data-free provenance signal that supports both correspondence recovery and lineage verification, and that this signal is substantially more robust to distillation than activation- and decision-boundary baselines. Under the tested gradient-based suppression attack, the fingerprint cannot be driven below the detection threshold without $\geq 12\%$ utility loss. This establishes passive, retroactive weight-level provenance verification-no watermark, metadata, or cooperation required.

\section*{Reproducibility Statement}

All results in this paper are produced by deterministic scripts with fixed seeds. The 52-pair MLP lineage benchmark (Tables~\ref{tab:survival}, \ref{tab:baselines}) is constructed from $2$ trained reference checkpoints, each paired with $5$ related-checkpoint kinds $\times 6$ derivative seeds $=30$ truly-related pairs (fine-tune, fine-tune to a new target, $\sigma_{\text{rel}} \in [0.01,0.15]$ Gaussian noise, $10$--$85\%$ magnitude pruning, and $16$--$256$-level uniform quantization), plus $16$ different-seed same-task pairs and $6$ distilled-student pairs as unrelated checkpoints. The expanded 159-pair set used in Figure~\ref{fig:lineage-three-panel} draws from the full $24$-checkpoint MLP zoo with the same transformation grid. Exact parameters are listed in Appendix~\ref{app:experiments}; null calibration uses the $50$ different-seed pairs from Appendix~\ref{app:verification}. Real-architecture pairing (Table~\ref{tab:transformer-family}) loads public HuggingFace checkpoints. Code, configuration files, baseline implementations (aligned Frobenius, singular-value distance, weight cosine, CKA, SVCCA, IPGuard regression analogue), the per-pair score JSONs, and the plotting scripts will be released upon acceptance.
