\begin{abstract}
Open-weight model checkpoints are routinely fine-tuned, quantized, pruned, merged, and redistributed without reliable lineage metadata. Existing provenance tools are brittle in this setting: hashes fail after any weight change, metadata requires trust, and watermarks must be inserted before release. We study post-hoc white-box weight-lineage verification: given two checkpoints with compatible residual architecture, can we determine whether they share weight-level lineage after common checkpoint transformations? This is distinct from behavioral similarity: under weight-level lineage, distilled students trained from fresh weights are unrelated to their teachers, even when they imitate the teacher's outputs.

We show that residual-network training induces an intrinsic fingerprint in residual-branch products. Correctly paired branch products develop diagonal-dominant fingerprint, measured by a trace/Frobenius score, that is absent at initialization, disappears under incorrect factorization, and does not appear in non-residual controls. We use this signal to align blocks between checkpoints and compute a model-level lineage score from centered residual-branch signatures. Across GPT-2, BERT, LLaMA-2, Mistral, Qwen2.5, DeepSeek-R1, ViT, Whisper, and ResNets, the fingerprint recovers block correspondences with 91–100\% accuracy. On controlled lineage benchmarks, this model-level lineage score yields complete separation: related checkpoints produced by fine-tuning, pruning, quantization, and LoRA merging score high, while unrelated checkpoints, including independently trained models and distilled students, remain low. Unlike activation-based CKA and decision-boundary IPGuard, which track functional similarity, this provides a passive, retroactive provenance signal for white-box residual-network checkpoints.
\end{abstract}

The open-weight ecosystem has transformed how neural networks are distributed and reused. When Meta released LLaMA~\citep{touvron2023llama}, the community produced instruction-tuned variants within weeks. Platforms like Hugging Face now host hundreds of thousands of model checkpoints~\citep{huggingface2024}, many of which are fine-tuned~\citep{devlin2019bert,ouyang2022rlhf}, quantized~\citep{han2016deep,dettmers2022llmint8}, pruned~\citep{han2015deep}, or merged~\citep{wortsman2022model,ilharco2023editing}. This creates a \emph{model supply chain} where downstream checkpoints inherit weights from upstream models, but the lineage is often undocumented or intentionally obscured. Model reuse is now easy; reliable model provenance is not.

Existing provenance mechanisms fall short. Cryptographic hashes break after any weight modification. Metadata and model cards~\citep{mitchell2019model} require honest distributors and preserved records; they can be falsified or lost. Watermarks~\citep{uchida2017embedding,adi2018turning} must be inserted before release; they cannot be applied retroactively to already-deployed checkpoints, and no surveyed scheme proved robust against fine-tuning or pruning~\citep{lukas2022sok}. Behavioral tests that compare model outputs cannot distinguish weight inheritance from imitation: two models can behave similarly without sharing weights, especially after distillation~\citep{hinton2015distilling}. Activation-based similarity measures like CKA~\citep{kornblith2019similarity} and decision-boundary fingerprints like IPGuard~\citep{cao2021ipguard} inherit this limitation. We need a passive, post-hoc signal that reads provenance from the weights themselves.

We ask: given a reference checkpoint and a suspect checkpoint with the same residual architecture (e.g., both LLaMA-2-7B derivatives), can we determine whether the suspect inherited weights from the reference, even after common transformations like fine-tuning, quantization, or pruning? This is distinct from duplicate detection (which hashes solve until the first modification), behavioral similarity (which conflates imitation with inheritance), and watermark verification (which requires forethought). The question is whether shared weight ancestry leaves detectable structure after modification.

The answer is yes. We find that training itself leaves a characteristic fingerprint in the weights of residual networks~\citep{he2016deep}. In these architectures, each block adds a learned transformation to its input via a skip connection. During training, the input and output projections of each block align so that their product $W_{\mathrm{out}}W_{\mathrm{in}}$ develops diagonal-dominant structure that is absent at random initialization and does not appear in networks without skip connections (Figure~\ref{fig:hero}b). This structure emerges from the optimization process: it is learned, not an initialization artifact (Section~\ref{sec:diagonal-dominance}). Orthogonal initialization satisfies dynamical isometry by construction~\citep{saxe2014exact,pennington2017resurrecting} yet shows no diagonal structure before training.

We exploit this fingerprint for provenance verification. For each residual block, we compute the product of its input and output weight matrices and measure how strongly the result concentrates along the diagonal. Correctly paired blocks from the same trained model show high diagonal concentration; mismatched or unrelated blocks do not. The method is architecture-aware, respecting the specific structure of Transformer MLPs, attention paths, and ResNet bottleneck blocks (Table~\ref{tab:factorization}). To compare two checkpoints, we align their blocks, compute a similarity score across all aligned pairs, and threshold against a null distribution of unrelated models.

The fingerprint appears broadly across residual architectures. On public checkpoints (GPT-2~\citep{radford2019gpt2}, BERT~\citep{devlin2019bert}, LLaMA-2~\citep{touvron2023llama}, Mistral~\citep{jiang2023mistral}, Qwen2.5~\citep{yang2024qwen2}, DeepSeek-R1~\citep{deepseek2025r1}, ViT~\citep{dosovitskiy2021vit}, Whisper~\citep{radford2023whisper}, and ResNets~\citep{he2016deep}), correctly paired blocks recover correspondences with over 91\% accuracy (random-initialization baselines achieve below 9\%).

For provenance, we move from block correspondence to model-level verification. On controlled MLP and ResNet-18 benchmarks, related checkpoints (modified by fine-tuning~\citep{ouyang2022rlhf}, weight noise, pruning~\citep{han2015deep}, quantization~\citep{dettmers2022llmint8}, or LoRA merging~\citep{hu2022lora}) score high on our lineage metric, while unrelated checkpoints (independently trained models and distilled students~\citep{hinton2015distilling}) score low. This achieves AUROC$=$1.000 with 100\% true-positive rate at 1\% false-positive rate. Distilled students that replicate teacher outputs are correctly classified as unrelated because they trained fresh weights (Figure~\ref{fig:hero}c). On the public LLaMA-2 family, the method correctly identifies three real fine-tuned variants (chat, Vicuna, CodeLlama) as related and classifies four independently-trained checkpoints as unrelated, including a same-architecture from-scratch model (OpenLLaMA) that inherits no weights.

We contribute:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
\item \textbf{Intrinsic residual fingerprint:} We identify diagonal dominance in residual-branch products as a training-induced structural fingerprint, absent at initialization and in non-residual controls.
\item \textbf{Verification method:} We propose a post-hoc white-box procedure for extracting, aligning, and comparing residual signatures between checkpoints.
\item \textbf{Broad validation:} We validate across 12 architectures spanning 5 model families and 280+ checkpoint pairs (including controlled synthetic benchmarks and the public LLaMA-2 model family), achieving 91–100\% block correspondence and AUROC$=$1.000 on lineage benchmarks.
\item \textbf{Robustness:} The signal survives fine-tuning, quantization, pruning, and LoRA merging while correctly rejecting distilled students, unlike CKA and IPGuard.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Scope.} The method requires white-box access and matching residual architecture (same layer count and hidden dimension); cross-architecture comparisons are out of scope. It computes per-block matrix products in under 1 second at 7B scale. It does not replace cryptographic signing, metadata, or watermarking; it complements them for cases where those mechanisms are unavailable. It detects \emph{weight} inheritance, not \emph{knowledge} inheritance: distilled students are correctly classified as unrelated even if they replicate teacher behavior. The lineage score is symmetric, establishing whether checkpoints share lineage but not which is the ancestor. Under gradient-based suppression attacks, the fingerprint cannot be driven below the detection threshold without $\geq$12\% utility loss. This is a practical provenance primitive, not a complete legal solution.

\section{Problem Setting and Related Work}\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Weight-Level Lineage Verification}

We address the following problem. Given two checkpoints $A$ and $B$, both with compatible residual architectures and white-box weight access, determine whether they share weight-level lineage. The transformations of interest include fine-tuning, pruning, quantization, and low-rank adaptation.

We define two mutually exclusive categories. Checkpoints are \emph{related} if one derives from the other through some transformation $\theta_B = T(\theta_A, D, \xi)$, where $T$ may include continued optimization, compression, or adapter merging, $D$ is optional additional data, and $\xi$ represents randomness in the transformation. Checkpoints are \emph{unrelated} if they were initialized independently, even when they share the same architecture, training data, task, or output behavior.

Under this definition, a distilled student trained from fresh weights is unrelated to its teacher, even when it closely imitates the teacher's outputs~\citep{hinton2015distilling}. Model-extraction copies~\citep{tramer2016stealing} that replicate a model's function through query access are likewise unrelated: they share no weight-level lineage.

The verifier outputs one of three verdicts: \textsc{Related} (shared weight lineage), \textsc{Unrelated} (no shared weight lineage), or \textsc{Inconclusive}. The method solves two tasks: binary lineage verification (do these checkpoints share weight ancestry?) and block correspondence (which residual blocks correspond between checkpoints?). The lineage score is symmetric ($\mathcal{L}(A,B) = \mathcal{L}(B,A)$), so the method establishes whether two checkpoints share ancestry but not which is the ancestor.

We assume the verifier has white-box access to both weight sets, that both checkpoints share compatible architectures (same layer count $L$ and hidden dimension $d$), and that metadata may be missing or adversarial. The method does not detect shared training data, behavioral imitation without weight inheritance, or architecture family membership. It is not a legal ownership proof; it is a forensic signal for weight-level provenance.

\subsection{Related Work}

We organize prior work by the signal each method measures.

\textbf{Cryptographic and metadata provenance.} Cryptographic hashes verify exact byte-level identity but fail after any weight modification: the avalanche property ensures that fine-tuning, pruning, or even floating-point rounding produces an entirely different digest. Model cards~\citep{mitchell2019model} and experiment-tracking systems document provenance but require honest distributors and preserved records; they can be falsified or lost when checkpoints are redistributed outside controlled environments.

\textbf{Embedded ownership signals.} Parameter watermarks~\citep{uchida2017embedding} and backdoor watermarks~\citep{adi2018turning} embed deliberate signals during training for later extraction. \citet{lukas2022sok} found that robustness varies by scheme and attack; no surveyed method proved reliable against fine-tuning and pruning within realistic compute budgets. These methods require proactive insertion and cannot verify unwatermarked legacy checkpoints retroactively. Output watermarking systems such as SynthID~\citep{dathathri2024synthid} mark generated content rather than model weights, addressing a different question: whether content was AI-generated, not whether one model's weights derive from another's.

\textbf{Behavioral and decision-boundary fingerprints.} IPGuard~\citep{cao2021ipguard} extracts inputs near the classification boundary as fingerprints; adversarial frontier stitching~\citep{lemerrer2020adversarial} uses adversarial examples to mark decision surfaces. These methods measure functional similarity. They are not designed to distinguish weight inheritance from functional imitation: a distilled student may share decision surfaces while having entirely independent weights.

\textbf{Representation similarity.} CKA~\citep{kornblith2019similarity} and SVCCA~\citep{raghu2017svcca} compare learned representations using activation patterns on a probe dataset. These methods may regard a successful distilled student as similar, which is reasonable for their objective (measuring representation alignment) but not for ours (detecting weight-level ancestry). They also require data or activations, whereas our method operates on weights alone.

\textbf{Weight-space model comparison.} Direct weight cosine similarity, Frobenius distance, permutation-aligned weight matching~\citep{ainsworth2023git}, and model merging geometry~\citep{wortsman2022model,ilharco2023editing,matena2022merging} compare checkpoints in parameter space. On benchmarks where related checkpoints are mild perturbations of the reference, flattened weight cosine is a strong baseline. The contribution of our work is not that nearby checkpoints can be detected in weight space. It is that residual-branch products expose an architecture-grounded, block-localized signal. This signal supports correspondence recovery, admits closed-form margin analysis, and remains competitive with the strongest data-free weight-space baselines. It also provides interpretable structure in terms of trained residual geometry.

\section{Training-Induced Diagonal Dominance in Residual Branch Products}\label{sec:diagonal-dominance}

Why should multiplying the input and output projections of a residual branch reveal any stable structure? This section answers that question by defining the residual branch product, presenting the observed decomposition in trained networks, offering a mechanistic hypothesis, and deriving the detection margin.

\subsection{Residual Branch Factorization}

A residual block computes $x_{\ell+1} = x_\ell + F_\ell(x_\ell)$, where $F_\ell$ is the residual branch. For a branch with $K$ linear layers $W_1, \ldots, W_K$ interleaved with nonlinearities, we define the \emph{residual branch product}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:branch-product}
M_\ell = W_K W_{K-1} \cdots W_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}
\end{equation}
The product $M_\ell$ maps the residual stream back into its own coordinate space, making its diagonal structure meaningful. Individual factors $W_k$ are rectangular and do not live in the same input-output basis; their composed product does.

The exact factorization is architecture-dependent (Table~\ref{tab:factorization}). Using the correct product is critical; Section~\ref{sec:experiments} tests whether incorrect factorizations degrade pairing performance.

\subsection{The Observed Decomposition}

In trained residual networks, the branch product takes a characteristic form:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:decomp}
M_\ell = -\varepsilon_\ell I + E_\ell, \quad \text{where } \varepsilon_\ell = \frac{|\mathrm{tr}(M_\ell)|}{d} \text{ and } \mathrm{tr}(E_\ell) = 0
\end{equation}
The term $-\varepsilon_\ell I$ is a generic component shared across trained residual blocks: it represents the block's contribution to an approximate identity mapping along the residual stream. The term $E_\ell$ is block-specific and checkpoint-specific; it captures the learned deviation from pure diagonal structure.

In many trained branches, the dominant trace component is negative. The verification statistic uses trace magnitude, so the method does not require a universal sign assumption.

\subsection{Why Residual Architecture Encourages This Structure}

The skip connection in $x_{\ell+1} = x_\ell + F_\ell(x_\ell)$ places the learned branch in an identity-centered parameterization. Training optimizes a residual correction around an explicit identity path rather than learning the full transformation from scratch.

For stable gradient flow, the Jacobian $J = I + J_F$ should remain near-orthogonal throughout training, a condition called dynamical isometry~\citep{pennington2017resurrecting}. If $J \approx I$, then $J_F \approx 0$; for small but nonzero learned corrections, the linear component of $J_F$ tends toward $-\varepsilon I$ with small $\varepsilon$.

The input and output projections $W_{\mathrm{in}}$ and $W_{\mathrm{out}}$ receive coupled gradients through the branch. This coupling aligns their product $M = W_{\mathrm{out}} W_{\mathrm{in}}$ with coordinate-preserving directions. Non-residual networks lack this constraint: without the skip connection, there is no functional pressure for the branch to approximate identity, and no mechanism couples $W_{\mathrm{in}}$ with $W_{\mathrm{out}}$ more than with any other weight matrix.

This is a mechanistic hypothesis, not a proven theorem. Section~\ref{sec:experiments} tests its predictions empirically.

\subsection{Margin Analysis}

We define the \emph{diagonal-dominance score} for a branch product $M$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dd-score}
s(M) = \frac{|\mathrm{tr}(M)|}{\|M\|_F}
\end{equation}
This score measures how much of the matrix energy concentrates on the diagonal. A matrix with energy uniformly spread achieves $s = \Theta(1/\sqrt{d})$; a matrix proportional to identity achieves $s = \sqrt{d}$.

The following proposition characterizes the detection margin once the decomposition $M = -\varepsilon I + E$ holds. It does not derive the decomposition from gradient descent; it quantifies the score induced by an identity-aligned trace component.

\begin{proposition}[Margin Formula]\label{prop:margin}
Let $M = -\varepsilon I + E$ with $\mathrm{tr}(E) = 0$. Then:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:margin-formula}
s(M) = \frac{\sqrt{d}}{\sqrt{1 + \|E\|_F^2 / (\varepsilon^2 d)}} \leq \sqrt{d}
\end{equation}
with equality if and only if $E = 0$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
We have $|\mathrm{tr}(M)| = \varepsilon d$ and $\|M\|_F^2 = \varepsilon^2 d + \|E\|_F^2$ (using $\mathrm{tr}(E) = 0$). Substituting: $s(M) = \varepsilon d / \sqrt{\varepsilon^2 d + \|E\|_F^2} = \sqrt{d}/\sqrt{1 + \|E\|_F^2/(\varepsilon^2 d)}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Signal-to-Baseline]\label{cor:baseline}
For mismatched pairs $(W_{\mathrm{out}}^{(j)}, W_{\mathrm{in}}^{(i)})$ with $i \neq j$ drawn from independent blocks, $\mathbb{E}[s] \approx 1/\sqrt{d}$. The signal-to-baseline ratio for correct versus incorrect pairs therefore scales as $d$.
\end{corollary}

The gap grows with hidden dimension: wider networks should produce more separable fingerprints. The theoretical upper bound $\sqrt{d}$ may be loose in practice if $\|E\|_F / (\varepsilon \sqrt{d})$ shrinks with $d$.

\subsection{Testable Predictions}

The theory generates five predictions that Section~\ref{sec:experiments} will test:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
\item \textbf{Training dependence.} The diagonal-dominance signal should be absent at initialization and emerge during training. Orthogonal initialization satisfies dynamical isometry by construction but should show no pairing signal before training.
\item \textbf{Residual dependence.} Residual networks should exhibit strong correct-pair diagonal dominance; non-residual networks (PlainNets) should not.
\item \textbf{Factorization dependence.} The correct architecture-aware branch product should yield high pairing accuracy; incorrect or incomplete products should fail.
\item \textbf{Width scaling.} The separation between correct-pair and incorrect-pair scores should grow with hidden dimension $d$.
\item \textbf{Transformation survival.} The fingerprint should survive fine-tuning, quantization, and pruning as long as the trained coupling between $W_{\mathrm{in}}$ and $W_{\mathrm{out}}$ is approximately preserved.
\end{enumerate}

Detailed initialization ablations, PlainNet controls, and training dynamics are in Appendix~\ref{app:experiments}.

\section{From Diagonal-Dominance Fingerprints to Lineage Verification}\label{sec:verification}

Diagonal dominance alone identifies correctly paired trained residual branches, but it does not establish lineage: unrelated trained residual models may both exhibit diagonal dominance. This section develops the method for converting block-level fingerprints into model-level lineage verification.

\subsection{Block Correspondence via Cross-Model Pairing}\label{sec:pairing}

Given reference checkpoint $A$ and suspect checkpoint $B$, each with $L$ residual blocks, we construct a cross-model score matrix $\mathbf{S} \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times L}$ with entries $S_{ij} = s(i,j)$. For reference block $i$ and suspect block $j$, we compute the cross-model branch product and its diagonal-dominance score:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cross-score}
M_{ij} = W_{\mathrm{out}}^{B,j} W_{\mathrm{in}}^{A,i}, \quad s(i,j) = \frac{|\mathrm{tr}(M_{ij})|}{\|M_{ij}\|_F}
\end{equation}
If $B$ descends from $A$ and block $j$ corresponds to block $i$, then $M_{ij}$ inherits the diagonal-dominant structure of the original branch product: corresponding pairs should score $\Theta(\sqrt{d})$ while non-corresponding pairs remain at the baseline $\Theta(1/\sqrt{d})$.

We recover correspondences via the Hungarian algorithm~\citep{kuhn1955hungarian}, which finds the one-to-one assignment maximizing total score:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hungarian}
\pi^* = \arg\max_{\pi \in \mathcal{P}_L} \sum_{i=1}^{L} s(i, \pi(i))
\end{equation}
For derived models with preserved block ordering, the optimal assignment should recover the identity permutation. Hungarian matching serves three purposes: it tests whether the signal identifies block identity independently of metadata, it handles potential block reorderings, and it produces a diagnostic correspondence matrix for inspection.

\subsection{Centered Residual Signatures}\label{sec:lineage-score}

Block pairing uses diagonal dominance to establish correspondences. Lineage verification requires a different signal: the trace term $-\varepsilon I$ is shared broadly across trained residual models and therefore useful for pairing but insufficient for ancestry. Centering removes this generic component, leaving checkpoint-specific residual geometry.

For each branch $\ell$, we compute a \emph{centered residual signature}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:centered-fingerprint}
R_\ell = M_\ell - \tfrac{\mathrm{tr}(M_\ell)}{d} I, \quad
\phi_\ell = \tfrac{\mathrm{vec}(R_\ell)}{\|\mathrm{vec}(R_\ell)\|_2}
\end{equation}
The matrix $R_\ell$ is exactly the $E_\ell$ term from the decomposition $M_\ell = -\varepsilon_\ell I + E_\ell$. The normalized vector $\phi_\ell$ captures block-specific and checkpoint-specific structure that can be inherited through weight-preserving transformations but is absent in independently trained models.

\subsection{Model-Level Lineage Score}

The lineage score aggregates similarity across aligned branches:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lineage-score}
\mathcal{L}(A, B) = \frac{1}{L} \sum_{\ell=1}^{L} \langle \phi^A_\ell, \phi^B_{\pi^*(\ell)} \rangle
\end{equation}
This score has several useful properties. Self-similarity: $\mathcal{L}(A,A) = 1$. Symmetry: $\mathcal{L}(A,B) = \mathcal{L}(B,A)$ when the alignment procedure is symmetric. Boundedness: $\mathcal{L} \in [-1,1]$ since each term is a cosine similarity.

Related checkpoints should score near $\mathcal{L} \approx 1$ because the centered residual signatures are inherited through fine-tuning, quantization, and pruning. Unrelated checkpoints should score near $\mathcal{L} \approx 0$ because independently initialized weights produce uncorrelated $E_\ell$ terms. Section~\ref{sec:experiments} tests these predictions.

\subsection{Verification Protocol}

To produce a verdict, we calibrate the lineage score against a null distribution of unrelated models. Let $\mathcal{N}_A = \{\mathcal{L}(A, U_j)\}$ be scores between reference $A$ and independently trained models $U_j$. The z-score is:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:zscore}
Z(A,B) = \frac{\mathcal{L}(A,B) - \mu_0}{\sigma_0}
\end{equation}
where $\mu_0$ and $\sigma_0$ are the mean and standard deviation of the null distribution. The protocol outputs:
\begin{equation}
V(A,B) = \begin{cases}
\textsc{Related} & \text{if } Z > \tau_{\text{upper}} \\
\textsc{Unrelated} & \text{if } Z < \tau_{\text{lower}} \\
\textsc{Inconclusive} & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
With symmetric thresholds $\tau_{\text{upper}} = 3.0$ and $\tau_{\text{lower}} = -3.0$, the false positive rate under a Gaussian null is below $0.13\%$. The protocol also checks architecture compatibility: if $\mathrm{arch}(A) \neq \mathrm{arch}(B)$, it returns \textsc{Incompatible}.

\subsection{Invariances and Expected Robustness}

The branch product $M = W_{\mathrm{out}} W_{\mathrm{in}}$ is exactly invariant under two function-preserving transformations.

\textbf{Hidden permutation.} If $W_{\mathrm{in}} \leftarrow P W_{\mathrm{in}}$ and $W_{\mathrm{out}} \leftarrow W_{\mathrm{out}} P^{-1}$ for permutation matrix $P$, then $M$ is unchanged.

\textbf{Reciprocal rescaling.} If $W_{\mathrm{in}} \leftarrow c W_{\mathrm{in}}$ and $W_{\mathrm{out}} \leftarrow W_{\mathrm{out}} / c$ for scalar $c \neq 0$, then $M$ is unchanged.

The method should degrade gracefully under fine-tuning (gradual drift in $E_\ell$), quantization (bounded perturbation), moderate pruning (partial coordinate destruction), and LoRA merging (low-rank update to inherited weights). Independent reinitialization, fresh training, and distillation should produce $\mathcal{L} \approx 0$. Section~\ref{sec:experiments} quantifies these predictions.

\subsection{Complexity and Requirements}

For a model with $L$ layers and hidden dimension $d$, the computational cost is dominated by score matrix construction: $O(L^2 d^2 h)$ where $h$ is the feedforward dimension. The Hungarian algorithm adds $O(L^3)$, negligible for typical depths. Memory requirement is $O(L^2 + d^2)$ floats.

The method requires weight access only; no activations, data, or forward passes are needed. Both checkpoints must share the same layer count $L$ and hidden dimension $d$. Full protocol details are in Appendix~\ref{app:verification}.

\subsection{Comparison with Modern Baselines}\label{sec:exp-baselines}

We compare against representation-similarity methods (CKA~\citep{kornblith2019similarity}, SVCCA~\citep{raghu2017svcca}), weight-space baselines (aligned Frobenius via Hungarian alignment, singular-value distance, weight cosine), and a decision-boundary baseline (IPGuard~\citep{cao2021ipguard}, adapted for regression via quantile-bin agreement) on a 52-pair MLP lineage benchmark (five related-checkpoint transform kinds plus different-seed and distilled negatives; per-kind composition in App.~\ref{app:per-kind-baselines}) (Table~\ref{tab:baselines}). Four data-free weight-space methods-including ours, weight cosine, and aligned Frobenius-reach AUROC$=$1.000 on this benchmark, so the headline number cannot itself distinguish them. The standardized Gap-$Z$ column makes the fragility of the non-weight-space methods explicit: IPGuard ranks truly related checkpoints \emph{below} distilled unrelated ones (Gap-$Z=-3.5$), reproducing the structural distillation failure of decision-boundary methods; CKA's Gap-$Z=+8.6$ is large in standard-deviation units only because $\sigma_{\text{distill}}=0.014$ is tiny, while the underlying raw ratio is $0.999/0.827 \approx 1.21\times$, so any benchmark reweighted toward distillation would push CKA's AUROC well below $1.000$. We do not claim that diagonal dominance beats weight cosine on aggregate AUROC; on a benchmark where related checkpoints are mild perturbations of the reference, a flattened cosine is a strong baseline. The contribution is rather that the residual-product signature (i) yields per-block correspondence as a by-product (only aligned Frobenius shares this; weight cosine and the activation methods do not), (ii) admits the closed-form margin analysis of Proposition~\ref{prop:margin}, and (iii) is competitive with the strongest data-free weight-space baselines while remaining interpretable in terms of trained residual geometry. The harder synthetic regime of Appendix~\ref{app:harder-bench} (layer grafts and linear merges) reinforces this positioning: three of the four data-free weight-space methods including ours (diagonal dominance, aligned Frobenius, weight cosine) remain at or near AUROC$=$1.000, while singular-value distance fails the merge regime ($0.448$) because spectral statistics are not linear in the weight-blend mixture, CKA drops to $\approx 0.80$, and IPGuard collapses (AUROC $0.05$ on grafts). The diagonal-dominance score additionally retains a near-perfect Spearman correlation ($\rho \approx 0.96$) with the fraction of reference blocks present in the suspect, supporting the interpretability claim that it tracks partial overlap monotonically rather than merely clearing a threshold.

\subsection{Public Model Validation: LLaMA-2 Family}\label{sec:exp-llama}

To move beyond controlled benchmarks, we evaluate lineage detection on the public LLaMA-2 family against the weight-space and decision-boundary baselines of Section~\ref{sec:exp-baselines} (Appendix~\ref{app:real-llm}). Using LLaMA-2-7B as reference, we score three real fine-tuned variants---the RLHF chat model ($\mathcal{L}=0.995$), the Vicuna-7B instruction fine-tune ($\mathcal{L}=0.996$), and CodeLlama-7B ($\mathcal{L}=0.336$), a code-specialized continued-pretraining derivative that is the weakest related checkpoint but still ${\sim}700\times$ above the null---against four independently-trained negatives: Mistral-7B, Yi-6B, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-8B, and, critically, OpenLLaMA-7B, a from-scratch reproduction of the LLaMA architecture that shares its exact $d{=}4096$, $L{=}32$ shape but inherits no weights. All four negatives score $\mathcal{L}\in[3.5,4.8]\times10^{-4}$, defining a real cross-model null ($\mu_0=4.2\times10^{-4}$); with only four real independents this is a descriptive separation check, not a calibrated false-positive rate. Diagonal dominance separates related from unrelated checkpoints by ${\approx}1850\times$ in mean score, versus $6\times$ for weight cosine on all seven pairs and $2\times$ for IPGuard on the logit subset where it is defined; aligned Frobenius and singular-value distance fail to separate this real-model set (AUROC$=$0.667), and the activation baselines (CKA/SVCCA) are omitted at 7B for cost. The OpenLLaMA case is decisive: identical architecture, zero inherited weights, correctly classified as unrelated---the verdict comes from the weights, not the configuration. This provides descriptive evidence that the margin advantage observed on controlled benchmarks transfers to real 7B-scale foundation models. Details in Appendix~\ref{app:real-llm}.

\section{Robustness and Boundary Cases}\label{sec:boundaries}

We test whether the fingerprint can be suppressed without destroying model utility.

\textbf{Function-preserving symmetries.} Per-block permutations ($W_{\mathrm{in}} \leftarrow P W_{\mathrm{in}}$, $W_{\mathrm{out}} \leftarrow W_{\mathrm{out}} P^\top$) and cross-layer rescaling ($W_{\mathrm{in}} \leftarrow c W_{\mathrm{in}}$, $W_{\mathrm{out}} \leftarrow W_{\mathrm{out}}/c$) leave the branch product $M = W_{\mathrm{out}} W_{\mathrm{in}}$ exactly unchanged, so $\mathcal{L} = 1.0$ under these attacks. Orthogonal rotations also preserve $M$ but break model function.

\textbf{Gradient-based suppression.} An adversary optimizes $\Delta$ to minimize $\mathcal{L}$ subject to utility preservation. Sweeping the utility weight $\lambda$ traces a Pareto frontier: with $\lambda \ge 1$, the attacker cannot push $\mathcal{L}$ below $0.91$; suppressing to chance level ($\mathcal{L} \approx 0.08$) requires $\ge$12\% eval-loss degradation; full suppression destroys the model ($57\times$ loss increase). Details in Appendix~\ref{app:attacks}.

\textbf{Distillation boundary.} The fingerprint tracks weight inheritance, not functional imitation. Distilled students ($\mathcal{L} = 0.086$) are correctly classified as unrelated in our same-architecture controlled benchmark---they trained fresh weights and share no residual structure with the teacher, even though they replicate its outputs. Cross-architecture distillation (e.g., BERT $\to$ DistilBERT) remains out of scope.

\textbf{Scope.} The method requires white-box access and residual architecture. Non-residual networks lack the identity-target constraint that creates the fingerprint.

\textbf{Weight-level, not behavioral.} The signature is a property of weights, not function. Distilled students ($\mathcal{L} \approx 0.08$) are correctly classified as unrelated despite functional similarity. The method cannot detect training-data overlap or knowledge transfer.

\textbf{Symmetric, no direction.} $\mathcal{L}(A,B) = \mathcal{L}(B,A)$ by construction, so the protocol establishes \emph{whether} two checkpoints share weight lineage but not \emph{which} is the ancestor. Directional attribution requires either training-time metadata or comparison against a third candidate (App.~\ref{app:verification}, branching tree).

\textbf{Lineage validation scope.} Lineage AUROC is reported on the synthetic MLP zoo and a CIFAR-10 ResNet-18 benchmark, with real-architecture evidence from block-correspondence pairing on Transformer families (Table~\ref{tab:transformer-family}) and a 7-pair real-LLM lineage test on the public LLaMA-2 family against a real cross-model null (App.~\ref{app:real-llm}). The latter identifies three real fine-tuned variants---the RLHF chat model, the Vicuna instruction fine-tune, and CodeLlama---as \textsc{Related}, while four independently-trained negatives (Mistral-7B, OpenLLaMA-7B, Yi-6B, and a Llama-3-derived DeepSeek distill) all score $\mathcal{L}\approx4\times10^{-4}$ and form the null, giving ${\approx}1850\times$ mean separation versus $6\times$ for the strongest baseline. With only four real independents this is a descriptive separation check rather than a calibrated FPR estimate. The remaining gap is tree breadth: all related checkpoints here derive from a single base. Extending to additional real checkpoint trees (a Mistral or Qwen base with its own fine-tunes) and a larger negative set that supports a calibrated FPR is the natural next step. The per-block branch products fit comfortably in memory at 7B scale, so the gap is engineering rather than methodological.

\textbf{Attacks.} Function-preserving symmetries (permutations, rescaling) leave $\mathcal{L} = 1$ exactly-these are invariances, not vulnerabilities. Gradient-based suppression requires $\ge$12\% utility loss to reach the detection threshold; full suppression destroys the model.

\textbf{Positioning.} This complements watermarking: watermarks require forethought; diagonal dominance offers passive, retroactive verification for checkpoints never watermarked.

\section{Conclusion}

Training leaves traces. Residual-network optimization couples each block's input and output projections so that their product becomes diagonal-dominant ($W_{\mathrm{out}} W_{\mathrm{in}} \approx -\varepsilon I + E$), with a theoretical $\sqrt{d}$ upper bound and an empirical $d^{0.87}$ growth observed from GPT-2 through LLaMA-2-13B and Mixtral-8x7B. Across 12 architectures spanning 5 families, the fingerprint recovers 91--100\% of block correspondences and achieves AUROC$=$1.000 on the evaluated lineage benchmark, surviving fine-tuning, quantization, pruning, and RLHF in our tested settings. Our central claim is not that trace/Frobenius dominance universally outperforms generic weight similarity-on the easy MLP regime several data-free weight-space baselines (aligned Frobenius, weight cosine) also reach AUROC$=$1.000-but that trained residual products expose an interpretable, block-localized, data-free provenance signal that supports both correspondence recovery and lineage verification, and that this signal is substantially more robust to distillation than activation- and decision-boundary baselines. Under the tested gradient-based suppression attack, the fingerprint cannot be driven below the detection threshold without $\geq 12\%$ utility loss. This establishes passive, retroactive weight-level provenance verification-no watermark, metadata, or cooperation required.

\section*{Reproducibility Statement}

All results in this paper are produced by deterministic scripts with fixed seeds. The 52-pair MLP lineage benchmark (Tables~\ref{tab:survival}, \ref{tab:baselines}) is constructed from $2$ trained reference checkpoints, each paired with $5$ related-checkpoint kinds $\times 6$ derivative seeds $=30$ truly-related pairs (fine-tune, fine-tune to a new target, $\sigma_{\text{rel}} \in [0.01,0.15]$ Gaussian noise, $10$--$85\%$ magnitude pruning, and $16$--$256$-level uniform quantization), plus $16$ different-seed same-task pairs and $6$ distilled-student pairs as unrelated checkpoints. The expanded 159-pair set used in Figure~\ref{fig:lineage-three-panel} draws from the full $24$-checkpoint MLP zoo with the same transformation grid. Exact parameters are listed in Appendix~\ref{app:experiments}; null calibration uses the $50$ different-seed pairs from Appendix~\ref{app:verification}. Real-architecture pairing (Table~\ref{tab:transformer-family}) loads public HuggingFace checkpoints. Code, configuration files, baseline implementations (aligned Frobenius, singular-value distance, weight cosine, CKA, SVCCA, IPGuard regression analogue), the per-pair score JSONs, and the plotting scripts will be released upon acceptance.
